How Hammond Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs such as discolored hot water, popping and rumbling, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and far less stressful than an emergency one with water on the floor.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, inexpensive repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water begins corroding the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and prevents a surprise cleanup.

Common Water Heater Problems in Hammond

The majority of water heater failures we see in Hammond start with the water itself. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes in the area still have the original tank, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod has usually been consumed and corrosion begins attacking the steel itself. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Well water can bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early.
