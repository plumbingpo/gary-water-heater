How Highland Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

A little routine care makes all the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so small issues get caught early.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the month and year it was built, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows discolored water, popping sounds, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, affordable repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Highland

Water heaters in and around Highland face a predictable set of problems. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A tank-style water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod is often eaten away, leaving the tank open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes shows up when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
